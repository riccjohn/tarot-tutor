Most confusion in reading a card comes from starting at the end, with the meaning. Start at the beginning instead. Each card is built in layers, and the order below follows how the layers were composed.

## Step one: the suit

Ask which of the four suits the card belongs to. This gives you the territory before you look at anything else. Swords is the mind at work: thought, argument, judgment. Cups is feeling, Wands is drive and will, and Pentacles is the material world of work, money, and the body's practical needs. The Major Arcana sits outside the suits and follows its own arc, so if the card is a Major, skip ahead to its place in that arc.

Suits carry an elemental attribution, and this is a genuinely disputed point. The scheme most readers meet pairs Swords with Air and Wands with Fire, and some older continental systems swap them. The site follows the first scheme and names the disagreement. Pick one and use it consistently, and expect to meet the other.

## Step two: the number or rank

Next, the number. Aces through Tens are a sequence, and each step has a character. An Ace is potential, a Two is a first pairing, and a Three is where a pairing meets the world and something has to give. The number tells you the stage the suit's subject has reached.

Court cards read differently. Page, Knight, Queen, and King are a ladder of maturity within a suit, so the rank tells you how the suit's energy is being carried, more than what stage it has reached.

Put suit and number together before you go further. A Three in the suit of thought is a different thing from a Three in the suit of feeling, and you can say something useful about the card already.

## Step three: the symbols

Now look at the picture. List what is literally there: figures, objects, weather, colors, direction of movement. Describe it the way you would caption it, without interpreting. Where the card is a clear image, this step is quick, and it is the one beginners skip most often.

Notice what is repeated, what is singled out, and what is missing. A background element that covers the entire scene means something different from one that touches a single figure.

## Step four: the common reading

Only now ask what the structure is conventionally read to mean. This layer is a lens, and one of several: the tradition offers a common reading, and you are free to take it, adapt it, or set it aside. It should sit on top of what you found in the first three steps, and it should be consistent with them. If the common reading contradicts the picture in front of you, trust the picture and note the gap.

## A worked example

The [Three of Swords](/cards/three-of-swords) shows the steps in order. Its suit is Swords, the territory of thought and hard truth. Its number is Three, the turn toward conflict, where something simple meets a third force. Its symbols are three identical swords crossing at one point through a single red heart, with grey rain falling evenly over the whole background. Only then does the common reading arrive: sharp emotional pain, or a truth that cannot be unseen. Every part of that reading can be traced back to a feature you already listed.

## Try it

Open any card and write one line for each step before you read the site's version. Then compare, and pay attention to where your line differs.
