A single card gives you one image to look at. A spread gives you several, and it tells you what each one is for. The layout is the useful part: it turns a pile of cards into a set of separate questions.

## Positions are questions

Every position in a spread carries a job. In a three-card spread, the first position might ask "what is behind this?", the second "what is at the center of it?", and the third "what is ahead?" The card you draw is the answer to that question, and no more than that.

This is the main thing to hold onto. The same card reads differently in different positions, because you are asking it different things. A Three of Swords in the "what is behind this?" position points at something that has already happened. In the "what is ahead?" position it points at something still to come. The card has not changed. The question has.

So before you turn any card over, say each position's question out loud, or write it down. Then read the card against that question, using the four steps from the previous lesson: suit, number, symbols, then the common reading.

## Choosing a spread

Fewer cards are better while you are learning. One card teaches you the steps, three cards teach you how positions work, and larger layouts mostly teach you how to keep track of a lot at once. The [spreads page](/spreads) lists the layouts on this site with what each position asks. Read a spread's position questions before you choose it, and pick one whose questions match what you actually want to look at.

Spread lineages are sometimes disputed, and the same layout name can come with different position meanings depending on who taught it. Where that is the case, the spreads page notes it. If you want to change a position's question to suit your situation, you can. Just decide before you draw, so the question is not shaped by the cards.

## Reading across positions

Read each card on its own first, against its own question. Only then look across them. A few things are worth checking.

- **Suits.** If most of the cards come from one suit, that territory is prominent. If all four are present, the situation has several sides.
- **Numbers.** A spread of low numbers reads as early stages, and a spread of high numbers reads as things nearing a limit or a conclusion.
- **Repetition and contrast.** Two cards showing similar symbols, or two that pull in opposite directions, are worth a sentence each.

Then write a short summary in your own words. One or two sentences is enough, and it should answer the question you started with. If it does not, that is worth noticing too.

## Keeping it small

A spread is a tool for looking, and it does not need to be exhaustive. You can lay out a layout and only read the positions that interest you today, and you can leave the rest face down. The reading is finished when you have said what you wanted to say about it.
