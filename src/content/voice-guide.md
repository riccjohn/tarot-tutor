# Voice guide

This file is editorial reference, not user-facing copy. It is not part of any
content collection and is not validated by a schema. Anyone drafting or
editing a piece in `src/content/` should read this first, and the Three of
Swords card plus the Swords suit and Three number essays are the worked
examples to imitate — they are the only pieces besides the spreads currently
marked `status: 'edited'`.

## The register

Plain and ownership-forward. Two failure modes bracket the target and both
are worse than sounding a little plain:

- **Mystical-earnest** — writing as though the mechanism is real and the
  reader should feel something. This asks for belief the reader doesn't
  necessarily have, and it reads as a pitch.
- **Skeptical-clinical** — writing as though the mechanism is fake and the
  reader is amusing themselves. This debunks the practice in the same breath
  it asks the reader to keep using it, which is condescending.

The way out of both is to say what the practice actually is, once, in plain
terms, and then get on with teaching it well. Something close to: a tarot
card's structure — its suit, its number, its symbols — was composed on
purpose, so reading it is closer to reading a deliberately-built visual
argument than to fortune-telling. What the reader does with that argument is
theirs to decide. State this once, early, in the voice guide and in the
about/intro copy. Do not restate it defensively inside every card page —
repeated caveats read as anxious, and anxious is its own tell that the writer
doesn't believe what they're saying.

## The two-layer structure

Every piece of interpretive content has two layers, and they are held to
different confidence levels on purpose:

- **Layer 2 — structure.** What suit a card belongs to, what number it
  carries, what is literally depicted in its artwork. This is
  textbook-confident. Write it the way you'd write a caption: declarative,
  no hedging, because it's either accurate to the source material or it
  isn't.
- **Layer 3 — the common reading.** What that structure is conventionally
  read to mean. This is hedged and invitational, always framed as one
  reading among many traditions and practitioners, never as the card's
  "real" meaning. It ends by handing interpretation back to the reader
  rather than closing the question for them.

Mixing the two — stating a conventional reading with the same flat certainty
used for "this card shows a red heart pierced by three swords" — is the most
common way this voice fails without tripping any schema check.

## Hedging rules

Hedge once per piece, not once per sentence. A single clear framing device
near the top of the interpretive material is enough:

- "One common reading treats this as..."
- "Readers who work with this card often see..."
- "A widely taught association is..."

Then write the rest of the reading in plain declarative sentences underneath
that frame — don't re-hedge every clause. A paragraph that hedges every line
("some might say... it could be... perhaps for some readers...") reads as
insincere, like the writer doesn't believe the sentence is worth finishing.
Say the frame once, mean it, and then write with the same directness you'd
use for the structural material.

Every card ends with an invitation prompt: a genuine, open question that
hands the specific reading back to the person looking at the card that day.
It is not rhetorical and it does not have a correct answer implied by the
rest of the copy.

## Contested points are taught as live disagreements

Where traditions genuinely disagree — Swords' elemental attribution is the
standing example, disputed between the Air-for-Swords/Fire-for-Wands scheme
most familiar from the 1909 deck's usual commentary and older continental
schemes that swap the two — say so directly in the content. "Some traditions
hold X, others hold Y, and this site follows X while naming the
disagreement" is honest. Picking one silently and presenting it as settled
is not, and an attentive reader who has seen the other scheme elsewhere will
correctly read the silence as sloppiness rather than confidence.

## Ethical guardrails

These are non-negotiable, not style preferences:

- **No death or illness framing.** Cards that are conventionally associated
  with loss, ending, or difficulty are written in terms of change, grief,
  disappointment, conflict, or letting go of something — never as omens of
  death, injury, or physical illness for the reader or anyone in their life.
- **No dependence-encouraging copy.** No streaks, no counters, no "you
  haven't read today's card yet," no language that manufactures urgency or
  guilt about use. The reader owes the product nothing.
- **No "you need today's card."** The practice is offered, not prescribed.
  Copy should never imply that skipping a day is a loss, or that the reading
  is required to have a good day.
- **Preserve the reader's agency.** Every interpretive claim is phrased so
  the reader can take it, adapt it, or set it aside. The invitation prompt
  exists specifically to make this structural rather than a nice sentiment.
- **No reversed-card meanings.** Reversal is taught as a technique — a
  separate, general-purpose lens applied on top of a card's upright meaning
  — and is never baked into a card's own content as a second fixed meaning.
- **Never name, quote, or cite a source book**, in content, code comments,
  or commit messages. Describe the deck descriptively when needed (for
  example, "the classic 1909 deck illustrated by Pamela Colman Smith") and
  never write the trademarked two-word name for it.

## Worked do/don't pairs

**Structure, stated plainly:**

- Do: "Three straight swords cross through a single red heart, entering from
  above and exiting below; grey rain falls in straight lines across the
  whole card."
- Don't: "Three swords seem to pierce what might be a heart-like shape, at
  least as this reading interprets it" — hedging a factual description of
  the artwork is just inaccurate throat-clearing. Say what's there.

**The common reading, hedged once and then committed to:**

- Do: "One common reading ties this card to sharp emotional pain —
  heartbreak, disappointment, or a truth that's suddenly impossible to
  unsee. That's one lens among several a reader might bring to it."
- Don't: "This card can sometimes mean sorrow, or maybe grief, or in some
  traditions loss, though of course every reading is different and this is
  just one possible interpretation among countless others" — six hedges in
  one sentence is worse than zero; it tells the reader the writer doesn't
  trust the sentence.

**The illness/death guardrail in practice:**

- Do (for a card conventionally tied to endings): "A common reading treats
  this as the end of a chapter — a relationship, a job, a way of seeing
  something — rather than a continuation. What it asks the reader to release
  is theirs to name."
- Don't: "This card traditionally warns of death or serious illness in the
  reader's life" — even when a source tradition frames it this way, this
  site does not carry that framing forward.

**Ending with agency, not a hook:**

- Do: "What would it mean to treat today's discomfort as clarity you needed,
  rather than damage you're managing?"
- Don't: "Come back tomorrow to see how this plays out!" — this manufactures
  a return visit instead of trusting the reading to stand on its own.

**The mechanism, stated once:**

- Do (in intro/about copy, stated a single time): "A card's structure — its
  suit, its number, its symbols — was composed on purpose. Reading it is
  closer to reading a deliberately built visual argument than to predicting
  the future."
- Don't (repeated on every card page): "Remember, this is just for fun and
  isn't real, but here's what it traditionally means, though obviously
  that's not actually true, but read on anyway" — restating the disclaimer
  on every page is the skeptical-clinical failure mode, and it undercuts the
  very reading it's attached to.
