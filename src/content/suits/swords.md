    continental systems reverse that pairing, giving Swords to Fire and
    Wands to Air. This site follows the Air-for-Swords scheme because it is
    the one the surrounding structural material assumes, not because the
    question is settled.

Swords is the suit of the mind doing its work out loud: thinking, arguing,
judging, cutting through, and sometimes cutting itself. Where Wands moves on
impulse and Cups moves on feeling, Swords moves on reasons — and reasons are
double-edged. A sword can defend a boundary or start a fight; it can dissect
a hard truth cleanly or turn on the person holding it just as easily.

The suit's forty cards (ten pips plus four court cards) trace what happens
when clear thinking meets conflict, at every stage from a single decisive
insight through to real exhaustion and, eventually, the kind of hard-won
clarity that only comes out the far side of a difficult stretch. It is the
suit most likely to be read as painful, because clarity is often
uncomfortable before it's useful — but the discomfort is the suit's subject,
not a verdict on the reader.

Traditionally paired with the element Air: intellect, judgment, and
communication rather than the will (Wands) or the emotion (Cups) most
readers reach for first. That pairing is not universal — see the note on
contested attribution above — but it is a useful way to hold the suit in
mind: Swords cards are asking what is true and what should be said, more
than they are asking what is wanted or felt.
